\begin{abstract}
The Apple Neural Engine (ANE) is the most power-efficient accelerator on Apple
silicon, but it is reachable only through Core~ML, which exposes no public
backward pass. We measure what it can and cannot do for deep-learning
training, on one Apple M4 Pro, with a reproducible harness and idle-subtracted
per-rail power. Core ML places no fp32 operator on the ANE; in fp16 it places
whole models on the ANE only above shape thresholds, and when it does the ANE
draws about 2.7~W while the GPU path costs 1.3$\times$ the latency and
2.8$\times$ the energy on the configurations where both are possible. For
training, the largest gain comes not from the ANE but from running the whole
step (forward, backward, loss, optimiser) natively on the GPU through MLX:
4--8$\times$ faster than a NumPy CPU path. A hybrid ANE-forward/GPU-backward
step never beats pure MLX on small models, because weight refresh requires a
model recompilation of hundreds of milliseconds. The ANE does win when the
weights do not change: fine-tuning a head on a frozen convolutional trunk runs
2.1--2.4$\times$ faster and uses about 10$\times$ less energy with the trunk on
the ANE than on the GPU, at the same accuracy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Training on Apple silicon is usually discussed as ``GPU or ANE''. In practice the
ANE cannot be programmed directly, the public stack gives inference only, and
frameworks that advertise Apple acceleration (MLX, PyTorch MPS) run on the GPU.
This paper asks three narrow, measurable questions: (i) when does Core~ML place
work on the ANE, and at what latency and power; (ii) how do Core~ML, MLX and
PyTorch-MPS compare against measured, not datasheet, peak throughput; (iii)
what does an ANE-assisted training step cost compared to a pure GPU step.
Contributions: an open measurement harness with clean-commit provenance; a
placement/latency/power characterisation of 576 Core~ML configurations; an
MLX-native training path for Energizer with a per-optimisation ablation; and a
measured hybrid-training overhead breakdown. All claims are tied to result
files in \texttt{docs/claims.md}.

\section{Background and Related Work}
Apple's Neural Engine (ANE) is reachable only through Core~ML~\cite{coreml};
MLX~\cite{mlx2023} and PyTorch's MPS backend~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} execute on
the GPU through Metal. Apple documents ANE-oriented transformer design
choices~\cite{apple2022anetransformers}, and community reverse-engineering
efforts describe ANE behaviour beyond the public documentation
\cite{hollance2020neuralengine}.

\section{Methodology}
Hardware: Apple M4 Pro, macOS 26.6.2 (25G83), Python 3.12, MLX 0.30.6,
coremltools 9.0, PyTorch 2.10. Every result record stores the environment and
the git commit; runs on a dirty tree are flagged. Latency is the median of
repeated warmed-up calls, with medians of repeats reported across repeats.
Placement comes from \texttt{MLComputePlan} (planned, not observed, device).
Power comes from \texttt{powermetrics} sampling at 100~ms per rail
(CPU, GPU, ANE); energy is reported net of a measured idle baseline. Machine
conditions matter: we observed 3--4$\times$ slowdowns on the GPU in two runs
that shared the machine with other work, and repeated those runs on a quiet
machine. Only runs that agree across repeats are reported.
Full procedure: \texttt{docs/REPRODUCE.md}.

\section{Characterisation of ANE Placement, Latency and Power}
Core~ML places no fp32 operator on the ANE (0 of 72 convolution, 24 matmul and 48
transformer configurations). In fp16 with \texttt{ALL} compute units, most
configurations are fully placed on the ANE, and the exceptions follow shape
thresholds (convolutions below roughly 128 channels at 64$\times$64, sequences
below roughly 128 at $d=512$ stay off). Planned placement matches observed
power: configurations planned on the ANE draw a median 2.7~W on the ANE rail,
those not planned draw 0~W. On 31 batch-8 fp16 configurations that can run on
both, the GPU path has a median 1.34$\times$ the latency and 2.82$\times$ the energy of
the ANE path (Table~\ref{tab:power}). \texttt{ALL} sometimes splits a model
between units and is then slower than a pure choice.

\section{Training Paths}
\paragraph{MLX-native step.} Moving forward, backward, loss and optimiser to MLX
cuts the Adam step from 8.9 to 1.9~ms (MLP, width 1024), 40 to 7.5~ms
(width 2048), 35.6 to 4.6~ms (CNN) and 29.7 to 7.2~ms (transformer) relative
to the NumPy CPU path. The ablation shows the optimiser is the largest single
factor: keeping only the optimiser on the host raises a width-2048 step from
7.4 to 23.7~ms; keeping only the loss on the host costs 3\%.

\paragraph{Hybrid.} Core~ML forward takes 0.2--0.7~ms and transfers 0.01--0.03~ms,
but the backward and optimiser pass on the GPU takes 0.4--0.8~ms and
refreshing the Core~ML model after a weight update takes 260--320~ms
(Table~\ref{tab:hybrid}). Hybrid never beats pure MLX at widths 256--1024.

\paragraph{Convergence.} On MNIST (MLP, 3 epochs, same seed) CPU, MLX and hybrid
fp16 with a weight refresh every step reach 93.00\%, 93.02\% and 92.98\%
(Table~\ref{tab:convergence}); the hybrid path takes about 135~s versus 0.2--0.4~s,
and most of its energy is on the CPU rail. Refreshing every 5 or 20 steps
diverged to NaN in our setting; this is a negative result of the
stale-weight approach, not of the ANE.

In the energy breakdown of the refresh-every-step run, the CPU rail accounts for
690~J and the ANE rail for 63~J of 737~J. A first run of the same experiment

\paragraph{Frozen trunk.} The hybrid fails because the ANE model must be rebuilt
after every update. If the trunk never changes, there is nothing to rebuild.
We pre-train a trunk on MNIST digits 0--4, freeze it, and train a new linear
head on digits 5--9 (Adam, 5 epochs, batch 256 unless noted). The trunk is
compiled to Core~ML once. We compare the trunk forward on the MLX GPU (fp32 and
fp16) with Core~ML on the ANE (placement 100\% ANE for every trunk), run online
every step, pipelined (the ANE predicts batch $i{+}1$ while the GPU trains the head
on batch $i$), or once into a feature cache (Tables~\ref{tab:frozen}
and~\ref{tab:frozen-cached}, Figure~\ref{fig:frozen}).

Every head reaches the same test accuracy whatever runs the trunk (within
0.05 points, fp16 trunk features within 0.1\% of fp32 for the Core~ML models and
at most 0.8\% for MLX fp16). The gain grows with trunk compute and is largest
for convolutions: on the widest CNN (64--256 channels, batch 256) the ANE run
takes 3.1~s against 6.6~s for MLX fp16 (2.1$\times$) and 8.8~s for fp32
(2.9$\times$), and 12~J against 136~J (11$\times$) and 214~J (17$\times$). The ANE
rail carries almost all of that energy and the GPU is idle. With
cached features the ANE extracts them 2.1$\times$ faster at 9$\times$ lower
energy; after extraction, all paths are identical. Pipelining the ANE with the
GPU head step adds 1.2--1.6$\times$ over the unpipelined ANE run, most when both stages
have comparable cost (MLPs, small CNN) and least when the ANE stage dominates (widest CNN, 1.2$\times$). For the small
MLP (width 512) the ANE gives 1.1$\times$ in time and 2$\times$ in energy, so there is
a size below which the ANE is not worth using for speed. The MLP heads reach
only 50--77\% accuracy because the brief digits-0--4 pre-training transfers
poorly through deep MLP features; their timing and energy are valid, their
accuracy is not a claim. Two independent runs (timing only, and with power) agree to
within 1--20\% on every configuration, and the same-commit caveat of
Section~\ref{sec:limits} applies.

\section{Conclusion}
We set out to learn what the ANE can do for deep-learning training. Starting
expectations were: (i)~that frameworks advertised as Apple-accelerated use the
ANE; (ii)~that the ANE would be the fastest place to run a model whenever Core~ML
allows it; (iii)~that placing the forward pass on the ANE and the backward pass
on the GPU would lower the cost of a training step; and (iv)~that this hybrid
would reach the same accuracy as fp32 training.

\paragraph{Answer.} On this hardware and software stack the ANE is an efficient
engine for \emph{fixed-weight, fp16 inference of sufficiently large operators},
not a general training accelerator. It cannot execute a backward pass, and every weight
update forces a model rebuild that dominates the step. It does help for the
part of training that does not change: with a frozen convolutional trunk it
doubled the speed and cut energy by an order of magnitude. The 4--8$\times$
training speed-up we obtained came from keeping the entire step, including the
optimiser, native on the GPU with MLX, not from the ANE.

\paragraph{What this does not show.} Larger models, other chips and later OS
releases may move the placement thresholds and the hybrid's break-even point.
The frozen-trunk test used one small transfer task (MNIST digits), trunks up to
about 31M parameters and a hand-written MLX forward that we did not compile with
\texttt{mx.compile}, so a tuned GPU baseline could narrow the gap. Pre-trained
models of realistic size (for example vision transformers) are untested.
Whether private interfaces would change the answer is left open; we used none.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
Single chip and OS build, one machine; Core~ML placement is a planner decision that may differ
at run time; no public ANE backward pass exists, so the hybrid path pays a full
recompilation per weight refresh; results are for small models and
inference-style forward passes; no private APIs were used. Large batch-8 matmuls (n\,$\ge$\,1024) varied by 1.4--5$\times$ between two full sweeps on the same commit, including pure-CPU rows, so we quote medians over many configurations rather than single large-matmul rows. The environment record of the power runs reports battery power although the machine was reportedly plugged in; we report it as recorded. Later runs record AC power and charge level in the environment. The frozen-trunk runs were made before their scripts were committed, so their result files carry a dirty-tree flag.

\section{Reproducibility}
All numbers come from raw JSON-lines files in \texttt{results/}, written by the
scripts in \texttt{experiments/} (one script per experiment, \texttt{make reproduce}
runs them all); tables and figures are generated from those files and never
edited by hand. Each file starts with an environment record: chip, macOS
build, Python, NumPy, MLX, PyTorch and coremltools versions, git commit and
dirty flag, AC or battery power, charge level, Low Power Mode and thermal
limits. The reference machine is an Apple M4 Pro (macOS 26.6.2, build 25G83,
Python 3.12.12, MLX 0.30.6, coremltools 9.0, PyTorch 2.10.0); the pinned package
list is in \texttt{environment/requirements.lock}. Seeds are fixed (data order,
initialisation, pre-training), MNIST is downloaded through torchvision, and timing
reports medians over repeated runs after untimed warm-up. Power and energy need
\texttt{powermetrics} (root), subtract a measured idle baseline, and are
reported per rail. Two runs were discarded because the machine was busy
(three to four times slower GPU paths); a sanity gate (MLX fp16 4096$^3$ matmul
near 23~ms) now identifies such runs. Energy and latency cannot be bit-identical
across runs; we therefore report spread where we have repeats and quote medians
over many configurations elsewhere. Code, raw results and the build recipe are in
the repository.
